# Causal ML for Continuous Treatments (Dose-Response) and Time-Varying Treatments / Treatment Timing in Continuous Time

Scope: thesis on Prescriptive Process Monitoring (PresPM) with IoT-enriched event logs. Original topic: individual dose-response for continuous interventions such as machine temperature. Refined RQ: decisions can be taken at any sensor reading, not only at events. How do we learn when and what to intervene, and how does decision granularity trade off against learnability?

**Verification legend**
- **[V]** = verified in this session. The title, authors and venue or arXiv ID were confirmed through a search result or a fetched page.
- **[S]** = secondhand. The entry comes from the researcher's prior knowledge, and its link or ID was not opened in this session. Most of these are canonical papers with stable arXiv or DOI IDs, but check the link before citing.

Research date: 2026-10-31. The searches covered literature up to that date, but recent coverage (2025–2026) is thin. See Gaps.

## (a) Continuous treatments / dose-response estimation

### Takeaway
The continuous-treatment literature runs from statistical foundations to deep learning. On the statistical side: the generalized propensity score (GPS), the doubly robust dose-response curve estimator of Kennedy et al., and DML with kernels (Colangelo & Lee). On the deep learning side: DRNet, then SCIGAN, VCNet, TransTEE, and the KU Leuven model CBRNet (TMLR 2025). Policy learning over continuous actions (Kallus & Zhou 2018; Demirer et al. 2019) and overlap/positivity are the open problems that matter most for prescribing a dose such as a temperature setpoint. All of this work is static, with a single decision per unit. None of it addresses decisions repeated over a stream of sensor readings.

### Cited Findings

**Foundations (GPS, statistical estimators)**
- **Hirano & Imbens (2004), "The Propensity Score with Continuous Treatments"**, a chapter in *Applied Bayesian Modeling and Causal Inference from Incomplete-Data Perspectives* (Wiley). It introduces the GPS, the conditional density of the dose given covariates. The dose-response function is estimated in two steps: regress the outcome on the dose and the GPS, then average over covariates. This is the base method that later neural approaches compare against. Relevance: the baseline for estimating a temperature-response curve. [S] — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/0470090456.ch7)
- **Imai & van Dyk (2004), "Causal Inference With General Treatment Regimes: Generalizing the Propensity Score"**, JASA 99(467). The propensity function generalizes the propensity score to non-binary treatments, and the method subclassifies on a low-dimensional parametrization of it. [S] — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214504000001187)
- **Kennedy, Ma, McHugh & Small (2017), "Non-parametric methods for doubly robust estimation of continuous treatment effects"**, JRSS-B. It gives a doubly robust pseudo-outcome that is regressed on the dose with local-linear kernel smoothing, and derives rates and bandwidth selection. It is implemented in the R package `npcausal` (`ctseff`). Relevance: a strong, interpretable baseline for the average dose-response curve. [S] — [arXiv 1507.00747](https://arxiv.org/abs/1507.00747)
- **Colangelo & Lee (2020+), "Double Debiased Machine Learning Nonparametric Inference with Continuous Treatments"**. Kernel-localized DML with cross-fitting for the dose-response curve and partial effects, with asymptotic normality. [S] — [arXiv 2004.03036](https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.03036)
- **Oprescu, Syrgkanis & Wu (2019), "Orthogonal Random Forest for Causal Inference"**, ICML 2019. Forest-based local Neyman-orthogonal moment estimation for heterogeneous effects that allows continuous treatments. It is implemented in EconML (`DMLOrthoForest`, `DROrthoForest`). [S] — [arXiv 1806.03467](https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.03467)
- **Wu, Mealli, Kioumourtzoglou, Dominici & Braun, "Matching on Generalized Propensity Scores with Continuous Exposures"**. GPS matching, available in the R package `CausalGPS`. [S] — [arXiv 1812.06575](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.06575)
- **Callaway, Goodman-Bacon & Sant'Anna, "Difference-in-differences with a continuous treatment"**. It separates level effects from causal response, the marginal effect of increasing the dose, and shows these can differ in sign. That distinction matters when tuning process parameters. [V] — [arXiv 2107.02637](https://arxiv.org/html/2107.02637v8); [moderndid docs](https://moderndid.readthedocs.io/en/stable/background/didcont.html)

**Deep learning dose-response**
- **Schwab, Linhardt, Bauer, Buhmann & Karlen (2020), "Learning Counterfactual Representations for Estimating Individual Dose-Response Curves" (DRNet)**, AAAI 2020. A multi-head network with a separate head per dosage stratum, which supports multiple treatments each with a continuous dose. It introduced the TCGA, News and MVICU semi-synthetic benchmarks. Code is on GitHub (d909b/drnet) [S]. [V] — [AAAI](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/6014); arXiv 1902.00981 [S]
- **Bica, Jordon & van der Schaar (2020), "Estimating the Effects of Continuous-valued Interventions using Generative Adversarial Networks" (SCIGAN)**, NeurIPS 2020. A GAN generates counterfactual dose-response curves, using a hierarchical discriminator. It is evaluated on TCGA, News and MIMIC. Code: ioanabica/SCIGAN [S]. [S] — [arXiv 2002.12326](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.12326)
- **Nie, Ye, Liu & Nicolae (2021), "VCNet and Functional Targeted Regularization for Learning Causal Effects of Continuous Treatments"**, ICLR 2021. A varying-coefficient network whose weights are spline functions of the dose, which keeps the curve continuous, unlike DRNet's blocks. A functional targeted regularizer makes estimation of the whole curve doubly robust. It introduced the simulated, IHDP-continuous and News-continuous benchmarks. [V] — [arXiv 2103.07861](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.07861)
- **Zhang et al. (2022), "Exploring Transformer Backbones for Heterogeneous Treatment Effect Estimation" (TransTEE)**. Attention over covariate and treatment tokens, covering continuous, structured and multiple treatments. It is a common modern baseline. [S] — [arXiv 2202.01336](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.01336)
- **Kaddour, Zhu, Liu, Kusner & Silva (2021), "Causal Effect Inference for Structured Treatments"**, NeurIPS 2021. Generalized Robinson decomposition for graph or vector treatments. [S] — [arXiv 2106.01939](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.01939)
- **Bockel-Rickermann, Vanderschueren, Berrevoets, Verdonck & Verbeke (KU Leuven), CBRNet: "Using representation balancing to learn conditional-average dose responses from clustered data"**. v1 (2023) was titled "Learning continuous-valued treatment effects through representation balancing". It was published in TMLR 2025. It applies representation balancing to continuous doses and adds a clustered-data benchmark where different doses go to different population segments. Relevance: KU Leuven group, and directly on point for the original thesis topic. [V] — [arXiv 2309.03731](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.03731); [TMLR/mlanthology](https://mlanthology.org/tmlr/2025/bockelrickermann2025tmlr-using)
- **Continuous treatment effect estimation using gradient interpolation and kernel smoothing (2024)**. It builds on VCNet and states overlap formally as every unit having nonzero density for every dose. [V] — [arXiv 2401.15447](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15447)
- **CCPFN, "Causal Foundation Models with Continuous Treatments" (2026)**. In-context learning of individual treatment-response curves. Only the abstract was seen. [V] — [arXiv 2605.15133](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.15133)
- A search also surfaced ContiVAE (a VAE for dose-response with unobserved confounders) and weighted-conformal prediction bands for continuous treatments. The exact IDs were not confirmed: one of the related links was [arXiv 2410.15706](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.15706v1). [V-partial]

**Policy learning / off-policy evaluation with continuous actions (prescription)**
- **Kallus & Zhou (2018), "Policy Evaluation and Optimization with Continuous Treatments"**, AISTATS 2018. Kernel-smoothed IPW and DR off-policy estimators for deterministic continuous-dose policies, with a bandwidth bias-variance analysis and policy optimization. Relevance: choosing a setpoint policy from logs. [S] — [arXiv 1802.06037](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.06037)
- **Demirer, Syrgkanis, Lewis & Chernozhukov (2019), "Semi-Parametric Efficient Policy Learning with Continuous Actions"**, NeurIPS 2019. Doubly robust policy learning when the outcome is semi-parametric in the continuous action, with regret bounds. [S] — [arXiv 1905.10116](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.10116)
- **Athey & Wager (2021), "Policy Learning with Observational Data"**, Econometrica. It includes infinitesimal nudges to continuous treatments. [S] — [arXiv 1702.02896](https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.02896)
- **Schweisthal, Frauen, Melnychuk & Feuerriegel (2023), "Reliable Off-Policy Learning for Dosage Combinations"**, NeurIPS 2023. Joint dose-response over several continuous treatments, with policy optimization restricted to regions of sufficient overlap. [V] (venue and content) — [aitopics record](https://aitopics.org/doc/conferences:813E24D9); arXiv 2305.19742 [S]
- **Vanderschueren, Boute, Verdonck, Baesens & Verbeke (2023), "Optimizing the preventive maintenance frequency with causal machine learning"**, International Journal of Production Economics. It learns the effect of preventive-maintenance (PM) frequency, a continuous or ordinal "dose", on overhaul and failure rates from more than 4000 industrial maintenance contracts, then optimizes the PM frequency for each asset. It beats a non-causal approach and a one-size-fits-all approach on semi-synthetic data. Relevance: the closest **industrial** application of continuous-treatment causal ML, from KU Leuven. [V] — [arXiv 2206.01562](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.01562v1); [Lirias](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/700482)

**Overlap / positivity with continuous treatments**
- Under continuous treatments, positivity must hold as a density: every unit needs nonzero density for every dose. Sparse regions of the covariate-dose space produce high-variance estimates. [V] — [arXiv 2401.15447](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.15447); [Schweisthal et al. 2023 record](https://aitopics.org/doc/conferences:813E24D9)
- **Jesson, Douglas, Manshausen, ... Gal (2022), "Scalable Sensitivity and Uncertainty Analyses for Causal-Effect Estimates of Continuous-Valued Interventions"**, NeurIPS 2022. Sensitivity bounds for continuous treatments under hidden confounding. [S] — [arXiv 2204.10022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.10022)
- Modified treatment policies and incremental interventions avoid strong positivity by shifting the observed dose (for example "set temperature to observed +2 °C") instead of fixing a value. See Díaz et al. (2021) and Kennedy (2019) under (b). [S]

**Open-source implementations (continuous treatments)**
- **EconML** (Microsoft) [S]: DML estimators (`LinearDML`, `SparseLinearDML`, `CausalForestDML`) accept continuous treatments and use treatment featurizers for nonlinear effects. Also `DMLOrthoForest` and `DROrthoForest` (ORF) and `DRIV`. Policy tools (`DRPolicyTree/Forest`) are for discrete actions only. [GitHub](https://github.com/py-why/EconML)
- **CausalML** (Uber) [S]: mainly binary or multi-category treatments (meta-learners, uplift trees). It has essentially no native dose-response estimator. [GitHub](https://github.com/uber/causalml)
- **causal-curve** (Python): GPS and TMLE dose-response curves for continuous treatments. [V] — [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/causal-curve/)
- **DoWhy** [S] (identification and refutation, with EconML backends), [GitHub](https://github.com/py-why/dowhy); R `npcausal` (Kennedy `ctseff`) [S], [GitHub](https://github.com/ehkennedy/npcausal); R `CausalGPS` [S].
- Author code [S]: DRNet (github.com/d909b/drnet), SCIGAN (github.com/ioanabica/SCIGAN), VCNet (github.com/lushleaf/varying-coefficient-net-with-functional-tr), TransTEE (authors' repo, linked from the arXiv page).

### Inferences
- All dose-response methods assume **one treatment decision per unit, made at a fixed time**. A temperature setpoint that can change at every sensor reading turns the problem into a time-varying continuous treatment. Here (a) and (b) meet, and the joint literature is thin. Exceptions: Soleimani et al. 2017 (continuous-valued and continuous-time), "Beyond the ATE … over Dose and Time" (2025), and modified treatment policies (LMTP).
- For prescription (not only estimation), the Kallus & Zhou and Demirer et al. continuous-action policy-learning results plus overlap-aware restriction (Schweisthal et al.) are the closest fit. The KU Leuven PM-frequency paper shows the full pipeline from causal estimate to optimized dose in an industrial setting.
- Positivity is likely a severe problem for IoT setpoints, since operators keep machines inside narrow ranges. Shift-type estimands (MTPs or incremental interventions) are a principled way to define learnable interventions under limited overlap.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed benchmark of continuous-treatment methods on real manufacturing or IoT data. All standard benchmarks are medical or text semi-synthetic.
- 2025–2026 neural dose-response papers were under-sampled. Beyond CCPFN (2026) and CBRNet's TMLR version, a focused search of NeurIPS/ICML/ICLR 2025–26 proceedings is needed.
- I did not open the ContiVAE and weighted-conformal papers, so their exact IDs and authors are unconfirmed.

## (b) Time-varying treatments, continuous time / irregular sampling, treatment timing ("when to treat")

### Takeaway
There are three strands. Classical g-methods: MSMs with IPTW, g-computation, SNMs, DTRs via Q-learning or A-learning, and their continuous-time counterparts (Lok; Røysland; Rytgaard et al.; Sun & Crawford). Neural counterfactual sequence models: RMSN, CRN, G-Net, Causal Transformer, TE-CDE, LipCDE, TESAR-CDE, Bayesian neural CDE, SCIP-Net, Observable Neural ODEs. Explicit treatment timing / when-to-treat policy learning (Nie, Brunskill & Wager, JASA; Hua et al.; Hızlı et al.; Bozorgi et al. for business processes). The continuous-time neural models (TE-CDE, SCIP-Net, TESAR-CDE) fit the "decide at any sensor reading" setting best. Most of them estimate outcomes under a given treatment sequence and do not learn the timing policy.

### Cited Findings

**Classical g-methods and DTRs**
- **Robins, Hernán & Brumback (2000), "Marginal Structural Models and Causal Inference in Epidemiology"**, Epidemiology 11(5). MSMs fitted with IPTW for time-varying treatments with treatment-confounder feedback. [S] — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1097/00001648-200009000-00011)
- **Murphy (2003), "Optimal Dynamic Treatment Regimes"**, JRSS-B 65(2). Semiparametric estimation of optimal sequential decision rules, the A-learning precursor. [S] — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9868.00389)
- **Cain, Robins, Lanoy, Logan, Costagliola & Hernán (2010), "When to Start Treatment? A Systematic Approach to the Comparison of Dynamic Regimes Using Observational Data"**, Int. J. Biostatistics. Compares treatment-initiation regimes ("start when CD4 < x") by cloning, censoring and weighting. This is the template for threshold-based when-to-intervene rules. [S] — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2202/1557-4679.1212)
- **Kennedy (2019), "Nonparametric Causal Effects Based on Incremental Propensity Score Interventions"**, JASA. It shifts the treatment odds instead of fixing a regime, which avoids positivity violations in long or fine-grained time series. Relevance: useful when there are many decision points. [S] — [arXiv 1704.00211](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.00211)
- **Díaz, Williams, Hoffman & Schenck (2021), "Nonparametric Causal Effects Based on Longitudinal Modified Treatment Policies"**, JASA. Shift interventions for continuous or multi-valued time-varying treatments, with a doubly robust sequential estimator, in the R package `lmtp`. Relevance: the cleanest formalism for a **continuous treatment over time**, such as temperature at each step. [S] — [arXiv 2006.01366](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.01366)

## Time granularity / discretization of decision opportunities (key for the granularity RQ)

### Takeaway
A small but established biostatistics literature shows that the choice of time discretization changes both the estimand and the bias-variance trade-off. Finer grids reduce discretization bias but increase positivity violations, variance and compute. Coarser grids mis-specify the data-generating process. Sun & Crawford (2023) formalize an **identification bias** that does not vanish with more data. Schulam & Saria show that discretization biases decision-making models too. Nobody has studied this trade-off for prescriptive or policy learning on IoT or event-log data. That is a clear novelty opening.

### Cited Findings
- **Sun & Crawford (2023), "The role of discretization scales in causal inference with continuous-time treatment"**. It defines identification bias, the gap between the continuous-time causal estimand and the estimand of a discretized treatment, and splits it into discretization bias and asymptotic bias. The bias can persist as the number of trajectories goes to infinity. The authors recommend choosing the scale carefully and running sensitivity analyses. [V] — [arXiv 2306.08840](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08840)
- **Ferreira Guerra, Schnitzer, Forget & Talbot (2020), "Impact of discretization of the timeline for longitudinal causal inference methods"**, Statistics in Medicine (DOI 10.1002/sim.8710). Simulation of pooled LTMLE for an MSM at different discretizations. It proposes cross-validated variance as a tool for picking the discretization. [V via repository and citation in Sun & Crawford; author list S] — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/sim.8710); [record](https://isidore.science/document/10670/1.4wicnl)
- **Sofrygin, Zhu, Schmittdiel, Adams, Grant, van der Laan & Neugebauer (2019), "Targeted learning with daily EHR data"**, Statistics in Medicine. Daily EHR data coarsened to 90-, 30-, 15- and 5-day intervals and analysed with long-format longitudinal TMLE for four dynamic rules. Guidelines recommend small intervals, but their feasibility and impact had not been evaluated before. Implemented in `stremr`. [V] — [arXiv 1705.09874](https://arxiv.org/abs/1705.09874)
- **Adams, Saria & Rosenblum (2020), "The Impact of Time Series Length and Discretization on Longitudinal Causal Estimation Methods"**. Compares IPW, iterative g-computation and TMLE across discretizations, with up to 257 time points in simulation and 73 in clinical data. Practitioners must balance bias against variance when choosing a discretization. [V] — [arXiv 2011.15099](https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.15099)
- **Schulam & Saria (2018), "Discretizing Logged Interaction Data Biases Learning for Decision-Making"** (arXiv only). Binning irregular time series into intervals introduces a "discretization bias" that degrades decision-making models. Continuous-time models avoid it. [V] — [arXiv 1810.03025](https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.03025)
- **Sun & Crawford (2022)**: the results of discrete-time g-method analyses can depend heavily on the discretization. [V] — [arXiv 2211.15934](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15934)
- **Ryalen et al.**: continuous-time MSM weights outperform discrete-time weights when processes evolve in continuous time. [V] — [arXiv 1802.01946](https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.01946)
- **Cox MSM simulation (PMC3947915)**: a discrete-time Cox MSM approximates the continuous-time one only if intervals are small enough that events are rare within each interval. [V snippet] — [PMC3947915](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3947915)
- **Process-systems causality pitfalls**: temporal aggregation or subsampling, unmeasured variables and **control loops** (feedback makes treatment endogenous) produce spurious causal conclusions in plant data. [V snippet] — [par.nsf.gov 10550040](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10550040)
- **Informative sampling** (TESAR-CDE): the observation process itself creates covariate shift. In event logs this means the granularity at which events are recorded is not ignorable. [V] — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/vanderschueren23a.html)

### Inferences
- **Granularity vs learnability, framed in this literature.** Finer decision grids, such as every sensor reading:
  1. reduce discretization and identification bias (Sun & Crawford; Schulam & Saria);
  2. increase the number of decision points, which multiplies IPW weights. That causes variance explosion and practical positivity violations (Adams et al.; Sofrygin et al.).
  Stabilized continuous-time weights (SCIP-Net, Ryalen et al.), incremental or shift interventions (Kennedy 2019; Díaz et al. 2021) and neural CDE outcome models (TE-CDE) are the existing tools that ease side 2.
- For PresPM with IoT, an estimand-level choice matters: deterministic "set temperature to x at time t" regimes versus stochastic or shift policies. The latter are far more learnable at fine granularity.
- A **novelty gap**: none of the discretization studies concern *policy learning* (when/what to intervene). None use process/event-log data or sensor streams, and none combine continuous doses with continuous decision times. Bozorgi et al. (PPM) decide only at events. Nie et al. and Hua et al. handle timing but not continuous IoT covariates at mixed granularity.
- Sun & Crawford's sensor-motivated framing, together with Zhang/Joffe/Small's "covariates observed only at discrete times", maps almost directly onto "continuous sensor stream plus discrete process events". These papers are strong theoretical anchors for the thesis.

### Gaps
- I found no study that empirically varies the decision granularity for **prescriptive policy learning** (as opposed to estimating the effect of a fixed regime). This is a likely novelty claim, but a targeted search in PPM venues (BPM, ICPM, CAiSE) should confirm it.
- I found no IoT or manufacturing application of TE-CDE, SCIP-Net, Causal Transformer or the when-to-treat methods. Industrial examples are limited to static dose (the PM-frequency paper), root-cause or causal-discovery benchmarks ([arXiv 2310.18654](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.18654)), and a 2026 PFN preprint.
- Unconfirmed: the author lists for Ferreira Guerra et al. and the Zhang/Joffe/Small paper, and the authors of arXiv math/0409436 (Gill & Robins vs Lok). Lok (2008), "Statistical modeling of causal effects in continuous time", Annals of Statistics, was not located.
- 2025–2026 coverage of Feuerriegel and van der Schaar lab papers is incomplete. Only SCIP-Net, IGC-Net and the 2026 ObsNODE paper were confirmed. Possible additional works (for example by Kacprzyk et al. 2024 on continuous-time treatment effects) were seen only as citations.
- Causal RL or optimal-stopping formulations of "when to intervene" on sensor streams (for example offline RL with confounding) were outside the searches run here and need a separate sweep.
